Understanding Straight-Line Geodesic Distance

Because the Earth is a three-dimensional oblate spheroid, the shortest distance between any two points on its surface is an arc along a Great Circle, rather than a flat Euclidean straight line on a 2D map.

a = sin²(Δφ/2) + cos(φ₁) ⋅ cos(φ₂) ⋅ sin²(Δλ/2)
c = 2 ⋅ atan2( √a, √(1−a) )
d = R ⋅ c

Where φ is latitude in radians, λ is longitude in radians, and R is Earth's mean volumetric radius (6,371 km / 3,958.8 miles).

Why is Driving Distance Longer than Straight-Line Distance?

A straight-line distance represents the theoretical minimum distance ("as the crow flies"). In practice, road driving routes are almost always 15% to 35% longer than the straight-line measurement due to:

  • Geographic Obstacles: Mountain ranges, rivers, lakes, and oceans require bridges, tunnels, or extensive perimeter detours.
  • Infrastructure Geometry: Road grids, highway interchanges, and residential streets cannot follow direct geodesic line-of-sight vectors.
  • Elevation & Switchbacks: Ascending or descending steep topography requires winding switchbacks that add road mileage.

Distance Units Comparison

  • Kilometer (km): Standard SI unit of geographic measurement (1 km = 1,000 meters).
  • Statute Mile (mi): Imperial/US customary unit used in the US, UK, and Caribbean (1 mi = 1.609344 km).
  • Nautical Mile (NM): International maritime and aviation standard based on one minute of arc along a meridian of the Earth (1 NM = 1.852 km).
