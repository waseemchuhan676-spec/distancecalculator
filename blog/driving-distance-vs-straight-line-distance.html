Driving vs Straight-Line Distance: How Much Longer?

Published on October 28, 2026 · Updated November 27, 2026 · 5 min read

When searching for the distance between two places, people frequently encounter two very different figures. For example, if you ask for the distance between New York City and Boston, one source might state 190 miles (306 km), while navigation software says 215 miles (346 km).

Both figures are correct, but they describe two distinct physical paths: one measures a straight line across the curvature of the Earth, while the other traces vehicular roadways.

What is Straight-Line Distance ("As the Crow Flies")?

Straight-line distance, also known as Great-Circle distance or orthodromic distance, is the shortest mathematical path between two points across the spherical surface of the Earth.

Because the Earth is curved, a straight line between distant points cannot be drawn on a flat map without distortion. Instead, navigators calculate the arc of a circle whose center coincides with the center of the Earth using the Haversine formula.

The Haversine Formula

The Haversine formula computes the great-circle distance from two latitude/longitude pairs:

d = 2R · asin(√(sin²((φ₂−φ₁)/2) + cos φ₁ · cos φ₂ · sin²((λ₂−λ₁)/2))), R = 6,371 km (3,959 mi)

The formula treats Earth as a perfect sphere. GPS uses the WGS84 ellipsoid instead (Vincenty's method); the two agree to within about 0.5% — about 9 miles on New York to London. Try it on our straight-line distance calculator.

What is Driving Distance (Road Network)?

Driving distance is the total length of road pavement traveled by a vehicle between two locations. Modern routing engines (such as Open Source Routing Machine / OSRM) calculate this by representing roads as a mathematical graph of edges (road segments) and nodes (intersections), factoring in:

  • One-way streets and divided highways
  • Speed limits and legal turn restrictions
  • Bridge crossings and tunnels
  • Bypasses around natural obstacles like lakes and mountain chains

How Much Longer Is Driving Distance, Really?

Automobiles cannot travel in a pure straight line. Driving distance is typically 15–25% longer than the straight-line distance on ordinary intercity routes (US Bureau of Transportation Statistics circuity factor 1.15–1.25). Measured on 13 popular US routes, the drive was 16% longer on average, ranging from 7% (Dallas to Houston) to 48% (Denver to Phoenix). Across 116 city pairs in Europe and Asia, the median road route was 23.5% longer, with half of all pairs between 16.5% and 32.9% longer (MapAtlas, routed 29 September 2026).

The difference between straight-line and driving distance is known in transportation engineering as the route circuity factor. The primary reasons include:

  1. Natural Geography: Waterways, estuaries, wetlands, and mountain passes force roads to deviate miles away from direct paths to reach suitable bridge or tunnel crossings.
  2. Man-Made Boundaries: Private property, national parks, urban density, and military zones necessitate perimeter roadways.
  3. Highway Grids: Modern interstate and motorway systems are built to connect multiple regional towns efficiently, rather than creating point-to-point direct links between every city pair.

How to Estimate Driving Distance from a Straight Line

Multiply the straight-line distance by 1.3 for a rough driving estimate — 1.2 for flat plains with direct highways, 1.5 or more in mountains. A global study of road networks found the average driving distance is about 1.3 times the straight-line distance, so 100 km as the crow flies is roughly a 130 km drive.

Worked example: New York to Boston is 190 miles as the crow flies. Multiply by 1.13 and you get 215 miles by road — 13.2% longer, below the typical 15–25% range.

When the Gap Gets Extreme

Sea straits, bays and mountain ranges push the ratio past 1.5×. In the 116-pair sample, the ten worst routes all had water or mountains on the direct line — Hiroshima to Matsuyama runs 2.87 times longer by road (67 km straight, 193 km driving). Short trips under 150 km also run higher (median 1.29×), because city streets and motorway ramps add fixed overhead to a short journey.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much longer is driving distance than straight-line distance?

On 13 popular US routes the drive was 16% longer on average, from 7% to 48% longer. Across 116 city pairs the median was 23.5% longer. Typical US intercity trips run 15–25% longer.

Why is driving distance always longer than the straight-line distance?

Roads bend around hills, rivers and property lines, and highways connect towns rather than running point to point. The gap is called the circuity factor: road distance divided by straight-line distance.

How do I estimate driving distance from a straight-line distance?

Multiply the straight-line distance by 1.3 for a rough estimate — 1.2 for flat plains with direct highways, 1.5 or more in mountains. In a 116-pair sample, multiplying by 1.2 landed within 10% of the real road distance for 83 of 116 pairs.

Can driving distance ever be double the straight-line distance?

Yes. Hiroshima to Matsuyama is 67 km as the crow flies but 193 km by road (2.87 times) because the route must cross the Seto Inland Sea. The two rims of the Grand Canyon are about 10 miles apart as the crow flies but roughly 200 miles by road.
