How to Calculate Fuel Cost for a Road Trip

Published on October 28, 2026 · 6 min read

Fuel is almost always the single largest variable cost of any road journey. Whether you are driving across the country or commuting between nearby cities, budgeting your fuel expense prevents unexpected financial surprises and helps you determine whether driving is cheaper than taking the train or flying.

The Core Fuel Cost Formula

Calculating your trip gas or diesel expense requires two basic equations:

1. Fuel Volume Required = Trip Distance ÷ Vehicle Fuel Economy
2. Total Fuel Cost = Fuel Volume Required × Fuel Price per Unit

However, the calculation changes depending on which fuel economy standard your car uses:

1. For Kilometers per Liter (km/L):

Liters Required = Total Distance (km) ÷ km/L
Total Cost = Liters Required × Fuel Price per Liter

2. For Liters per 100 Kilometers (L/100 km):

Liters Required = (Total Distance (km) ÷ 100) × (L/100 km Rating)
Total Cost = Liters Required × Fuel Price per Liter

3. For Miles per Gallon (MPG):

Gallons Required = Total Distance (miles) ÷ MPG
Total Cost = Gallons Required × Fuel Price per Gallon

Worked Example: Round-Trip Driving Vacation

Suppose you are planning a round-trip vacation from Los Angeles to Las Vegas:

  • One-Way Distance: 270 miles (435 km)
  • Total Round-Trip Distance: 540 miles (870 km)
  • Vehicle Efficiency: 30 MPG (US)
  • Fuel Price: $4.20 per gallon
Calculation:
• Gallons Required = 540 miles ÷ 30 MPG = 18.0 Gallons
• Total Fuel Expense = 18.0 gallons × $4.20 = $75.60

One-Way vs. Round-Trip Budgeting

A frequent mistake travelers make when estimating fuel budgets is computing one-way driving miles and forgetting the return journey. Always check whether your road trip involves returning along the same route (multiply distance by 2) or continuing along a multi-city scenic circuit.

4 Factors That Alter Your Actual Fuel Burn

Manufacturer EPA or WLTP mileage figures reflect controlled laboratory testing. Real-world fuel consumption will vary based on:

  1. Cruising Speed: Aerodynamic drag increases with the square of velocity. Driving at 75 mph (120 km/h) consumes approximately 20% more fuel than driving at 60 mph (95 km/h).
  2. Payload & Roof Racks: A loaded car trunk, bicycle racks, or roof-mounted luggage carriers introduce substantial wind resistance.
  3. Air Conditioning Usage: Running the A/C continuously in high summer temperatures adds 5% to 10% to fuel consumption.
  4. Terrain: Climbing mountain passes increases engine workload, though gentle descents partially recover momentum.
